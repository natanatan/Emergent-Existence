# 9.2 Formalism Is Not Ontology

The first discipline of the chapter is negative. A graph representing a relational domain does not make reality a graph. A matrix recording relations does not make our ontology linear algebra. A simplex realizing a closed incidence pattern does not make simplices primitive. A category organizing compositions does not turn objects and morphisms into substances.

▪ representation of X ≠ ontological identity of X

This distinction has run through the book. Formalism must make it explicit, because formal power increases the risk of ontological smuggling. Mathematics arrives in bundles. A vector space brings scalar multiplication and addition. An inner product brings angle and norm. A topology brings neighborhoods and continuity. A probability space brings a measure. None of that is free merely because a later theory may need it.

A formalism is admissible in one of three ways. It may directly encode structure already earned, it may define a new structure from what has been earned, or it may be used as external representational machinery while being denied ontological force.

▪ formal resource = inherited ∨ derived ∨ explicitly representational

Anything else is explanatory debt.
