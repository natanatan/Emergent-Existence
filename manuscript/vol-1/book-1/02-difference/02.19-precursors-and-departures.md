# 2.19 Precursors and Departures

The ingredients of this book are not new. Non-equivalence, transition relations, automorphisms, retention tests and information-theoretic comparisons are established ideas, and later chapters do not repeat the point. What the book proposes is architectural: placing them in one inheritance-tracked derivation, making each assumption cost explicit, and refusing later structure until earlier chapters have earned it.

**Precursors.** Spencer-Brown’s *Laws of Form* (1969) and Isaacson and Kauffman’s recursive distinctioning (2016) are close neighbors for the idea that distinctions, and recursive operations on them, can generate structured patterns. “Weakly discernible” is used only in its established philosophical sense: items discerned by an irreflexive symmetric relation without differing in intrinsic properties, as discussed by Saunders, Hawley, Ladyman, Linnebo and Pettigrew, and Muller.

**What is new here.** The chapter isolates symmetric dyadic non-equivalence as the least constructive stage in the sequence, withholds operative transformation from it, and records dyadicity as a primitive cost rather than pretending that weak discernibility generates multiplicity. The claim under test is whether this ordering can support the later derivation without importing succession, orientation, information, geometry or objects too early.

Questions Opened by This Framework

Can primitive dyadicity be reduced to a weaker recursive structure without importing numerical multiplicity in disguise?

Is symmetric irreflexive non-equivalence the weakest relation sufficient for the later architecture?

Can a physical theory ever distinguish co-constituted poles without eventually introducing intrinsic qualitative properties?

What is the exact boundary between a symmetry visible in a model and an operation available to the modeled system?

Does any candidate pre-spacetime physics supply evidence for dyadic non-equivalence at its foundation, or is the present proposal purely reconstructive?
