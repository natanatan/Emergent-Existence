# 12.19 Scaling Dimension and Fractal Geometry

Both dimensions earned so far are counts. d_R counts the independent modes an organization supports; d_probe counts the parameters a family of probes cannot do without. A count is a whole number. A third kind of dimension answers a different question: how does the amount of a domain that a frame must resolve grow as the resolving standard is refined? The answer is an exponent, and an exponent need not be whole. Fractal geometry is the mathematics of domains in which it is not.

This section teaches that mathematics on its own terms, with its standard definitions and standard examples. The framework uses it afterward only as far as the definitions license.

**Covering.** Section 12.2 gave scale-relative neighborhoods N_ε(A). Ask how many such neighborhoods are needed to cover a domain X, and write M(ε) for the smallest number. For an ordinary curve, halving ε roughly doubles M(ε). For an ordinary surface it multiplies M(ε) by four, and for an ordinary solid by eight. In each case M(ε) grows as a power of 1/ε, and the power is the familiar dimension. Reading the exponent off the count gives the box-counting dimension:

▪ M(ε) ∝ ε^(−d_B), so d_B = lim_{ε→0} log M(ε) / log(1/ε), where the limit exists

The construction uses only distances and the neighborhoods they define. It needs no coordinates (11.13) and no embedding (11.14), which is why it can be taught as soon as a metric is in hand.

**Self-similar sets.** Some sets are made of N copies of themselves, each scaled by a factor r. For these the exponent can be read directly:

▪ d_sim = log N / log(1/r)

The middle-thirds Cantor set keeps two copies at one third: d_sim = log 2 / log 3 ≈ 0.631, more than a scatter of points and less than a line. The Koch curve replaces each segment with four segments one third as long: d_sim = log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.262. It is a curve, yet the length between any two of its points is infinite. The Sierpiński triangle keeps three copies at one half: d_sim = log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585. For each of these sets the self-similarity dimension, the box-counting dimension and the Hausdorff dimension below all agree.

**Hausdorff dimension.** Box counting uses neighborhoods of one size. The rigorous definition, due to Hausdorff, allows covers by sets of any sizes. For a candidate exponent s, cover X by sets U_i all smaller than δ, add up diam(U_i)^s, take the smallest total any such cover achieves, and let δ shrink to zero. The result is infinite for every s below one critical value and zero for every s above it. That critical value is the Hausdorff dimension d_H. It is defined on any metric domain, and it never exceeds the box-counting dimension. The two can differ: the rational numbers between 0 and 1 have d_H = 0, because they are countable, but d_B = 1, because every interval contains some of them.

**Topological dimension, and what "fractal" means.** The topological, or covering, dimension d_T asks about overlap rather than size, the property 12.3 made the first collective geometric constraint. It is the smallest n such that every open cover of X can be refined so that no point lies in more than n + 1 of the covering sets. It is always a whole number, and stretching or bending a domain without tearing it does not change it. The Koch curve has d_T = 1 and d_H ≈ 1.262. Mandelbrot proposed calling a set fractal when its Hausdorff dimension strictly exceeds its topological dimension:

▪ d_T ≤ d_H, and X is fractal in Mandelbrot's sense when d_T < d_H

Mathematicians have not settled on one definition. Falconer recommends recognizing fractals by a family of properties: detail at arbitrarily fine scales, irregularity that ordinary geometry cannot describe, some form of self-similarity, possibly approximate or statistical, a fractal dimension exceeding the topological one, and often a simple recursive definition.

Where the familiar geometry of smooth regimes (12.12) holds, d_R, d_probe, d_T and d_B agree. Departure among them is the signature of something else. The framework keeps the two kinds apart:

▪ rank dimension (d_R, d_probe) is a count ≠ scaling dimension (d_B, d_H, d_s) is an exponent

**Four distinctions.** Four ideas are easily run together. *Recursion* is a property of a process: a rule applied to its own output. *Scale invariance* is preservation of some identified feature under a scale relation (10.21). *Self-similarity* is the stronger case, in which the organization at one level reproduces the organization at another (10.21); it may be exact, as in the Koch curve, or statistical, as in a coastline, where only the distribution of features reproduces. *Fractality* is a measured property of a geometry: a scaling dimension exceeding the topological one, or the family of properties above.

None of these implies the next without further conditions. A recursive rule can produce a perfectly smooth result: cutting the corners of a polygon again and again, by Chaikin's rule, converges to a smooth curve with Hausdorff dimension exactly one. And a fractal need not reproduce itself: the boundary of the Mandelbrot set has Hausdorff dimension two, and although small distorted copies of the whole appear throughout it, it is not built from exact scaled copies of itself.

▪ recursive organization ⇏ fractal geometry

▪ fractal geometry ⇏ exact self-similarity

**Scaling windows.** Mathematical fractals have structure at every scale. Physical and computed ones do not. Below some grain there is no finer structure to resolve, and above some size the domain ends or turns uniform. For any finite domain M(ε) stops growing once ε passes below the smallest separation, so the limit in the definition of d_B is zero. What can be measured is the slope across a declared window between a lower cutoff ε_min and an upper cutoff ε_max:

▪ d_B(ε_min, ε_max) = slope of log M(ε) against log(1/ε) for ε_min ≪ ε ≪ ε_max

Richardson found that the measured length of the west coast of Britain grows as the measuring rod shrinks. Mandelbrot read the growth as a scaling dimension of about 1.25, valid across the range of rods Richardson used and not beyond it. Galaxy clustering gives a larger example: over small and intermediate scales the distribution of galaxies shows fractal-like scaling, and surveys find a transition to homogeneity, where the scaling dimension reaches three, at around a few hundred million light-years. Both are bounded fractals: a scaling law holds across a window, and characteristic scales mark where it begins and ends.

▪ fractal geometry ⇏ scale invariance at all scales

Taking the slope locally, at each scale rather than across the whole window, gives a scaling dimension that depends on scale. This is the precise meaning of the scale-dependent dimension d_eff(σ) of 12.27. The local slope may hold steady, change in steps between regimes, or sit at a noninteger value across a window before settling to an integer.

**Spectral dimension.** A second scaling dimension measures how a walk spreads through a domain rather than how much of it a frame must cover. Start a random walk at a locus A and let p_m(A) be the probability that it stands at A again after m steps. On a regular d-dimensional lattice, p_m(A) falls off as m^(−d/2). Reading the exponent the other way gives the spectral dimension:

▪ p_m(A) ∝ m^(−d_s/2), so d_s = −2 d log p_m(A) / d log m

The walk length m is often called diffusion time; here it counts steps, since Time has not been earned. The walk also needs a rule that weights its steps, which is a probability measure, and Scale withheld one. The definition is therefore taught here as mathematics, and the framework applies it only where such a weighting has been earned. The name comes from an equivalent definition through the spectrum of the domain's Laplacian.

The spectral dimension can differ from the Hausdorff dimension of the same set. The Sierpiński triangle has d_H ≈ 1.585 but d_s = 2 log 3 / log 5 ≈ 1.365: it is spread out like a set of dimension 1.585, while a walk on it wanders as if in a space of dimension 1.365. On a smooth domain all of these dimensions coincide.

▪ spectral dimension ≠ Hausdorff dimension

The spectral dimension matters beyond this chapter because it is the dimension several approaches to quantum gravity compute for spacetime itself. In causal dynamical triangulations, and in asymptotically safe gravity, the spectral dimension is computed close to four at large scales and falling toward two at small scales. Book II returns to these results.

**What the framework gains.** Geometry now has a third kind of dimension, a scaling exponent measured over a declared window, alongside the two counts. Fractal geometry enters as a way of measuring, and the framework commits to no fractal ontology by adopting it. Recursive relational organization may produce scale-dependent geometric structure; whether that structure is fractal is a question for measured scaling. The framework allows both outcomes: regimes in which a scaling law holds, and characteristic scales at which it breaks.
