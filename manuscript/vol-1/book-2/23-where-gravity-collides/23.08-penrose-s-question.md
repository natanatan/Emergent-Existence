# 23.8 Penrose's Question

The slit detector explains why the stripes vanish. It does not explain the dots. Each trial ends with a single mark at a single place, and no account so far says why.

Standard quantum mechanics handles the dot with a rule added from outside its equations. Between measurements, a quantum state evolves smoothly and stays spread out. At a measurement, it is said to *collapse* to one outcome, with probabilities set by a rule Max Born proposed in 1926. The collapse is not described by the equations; it is stipulated. If a measuring device is itself treated as a quantum system, as it must be, the equations predict that the device too goes into a superposition of readings, yet every laboratory sees exactly one reading. This is the *measurement problem*, and after a century it remains unsolved.

One established answer needs no collapse at all. In 1927 de Broglie proposed, and David Bohm developed in 1952, that a particle always has a definite position and is carried by its wave, which passes through both slits and guides it. The dot marks where the particle actually was; the stripes come from the wave steering it. This *pilot-wave theory* predicts the same outcomes as standard quantum mechanics, but it takes particles and their paths as given, and its guiding wave acts instantly across any distance.

In 1996 Roger Penrose proposed that gravity solves it. Penrose's argument runs as follows. Every object with mass curves spacetime. An object in a superposition of two positions should therefore be accompanied by a superposition of two slightly different curvatures of spacetime, two geometries at once. But general relativity offers no common frame in which two different geometries can be compared point by point: the very notions of "the same place" and "the same time" are defined by the geometry. Two incompatible geometries cannot both be carried. Penrose proposed that such a superposition holds together only while the two geometries differ by less than a certain amount, set by how much gravitational energy separates them. That energy grows with the object's mass and with how far apart the two arrangements sit, up to about the object's own width. Below the threshold, as for an electron, both arrangements are carried and the stripes appear. Above it, one arrangement is retained, kept on record in the sense of Retention (Chapter 4), and the stripes cannot form. Objects approaching the size of a speck of dust are near the threshold, and anything we can see is far beyond it. The Hungarian physicist Lajos Diósi had reached a closely related formula a decade earlier.

Penrose's picture turns the problem around. Superposition is the default. Definite outcomes are what gravity forces when alternatives become too different in their effect on spacetime. Collapse is no longer a rule imposed from outside; it is a physical process, with a rate that experiments can test.

Experimenters have been testing it. In 2021 an underground experiment in Italy looked for the faint radiation that the simplest version of the Diósi–Penrose idea predicts, and did not find it, ruling that version out. In 2026 another underground experiment ruled out a generalized version of a related model proposed by Frigyes Károlyházy, whose original form had been excluded in 1993. Neither rules out the idea itself; each rules out a specific way of setting the threshold. The question of where the threshold lies, and what sets it, is open, and it is now the next frontier for researchers to explore.
