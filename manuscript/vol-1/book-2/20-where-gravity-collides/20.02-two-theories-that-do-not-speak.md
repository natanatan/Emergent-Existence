# 20.2 Two Theories That Do Not Speak

The split began around 1900, from both sides at once.

On one side, Max Planck proposed in 1900 that the color of light given off by hot objects could be explained if energy were exchanged in fixed, tiny packets. The size of the packet is set by a new constant of nature, now called Planck's constant, *h*. The constant is extraordinarily small: about 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds, a unit of energy multiplied by time. In 1905 Einstein proposed that light itself arrives in such packets, later called photons. Over the next two decades, Niels Bohr, Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrödinger, Max Born and others built quantum mechanics on that foundation. Quantum mechanics predicts the results of experiments on atoms to better than one part in a billion.

On the other side, also in 1905, Einstein took up a set of equations the Dutch physicist Hendrik Lorentz had written down to describe how measurements of length and time change between observers moving relative to each other. Einstein read Lorentz's equations as a fact about the world itself: two observers in relative motion would genuinely disagree about how long a ruler is and how fast a clock ticks, and the *Lorentz transformations* say exactly by how much. What the two observers would agree on is the speed of light, and a combined measure of separation in space and time. Henceforth, space and time became one thing, *spacetime*. Ten years later, general relativity explained gravity this way: mass and energy curve spacetime, and the curvature tells objects how to move. The theory has since predicted the bending of starlight, the slowing of clocks in gravity (now measured so precisely that clocks only a third of a meter apart in height tick at measurably different rates), black holes, and even gravitational waves, which were first detected in 2015.

Einstein helped found both theories, and quantum mechanics split its founders into two camps: Einstein, Erwin Schrödinger and Louis de Broglie on one side, and Bohr, Heisenberg and Born on the other. Einstein never accepted that quantum mechanics was complete. His long debate with Bohr, beginning at the Solvay Conference of 1927, turned on whether a measurement reveals a value that was already there, or whether the measurement takes part in producing it. That question is still open, and this chapter takes a side.

The technical form of the problem is easy enough to state. General relativity is a *classical* theory: its objects have definite properties at all times. Quantum mechanics is not. When physicists try to make gravity quantum in the same way as the other forces, the mathematics breaks down at a scale called the Planck length, about 10⁻³⁵ meters, far smaller than anything an experiment can reach. Most approaches to "quantum gravity" try to repair the theory at that scale. This chapter takes a different approach, the one Penrose considered: what happens when quantum superposition meets gravity at scales we *can* reach?
