# 20.17 Why Right Angles, and Why Exactly Two?

The electromagnetic requirement comes from Chapter 19:

$$\text{spatially independent} + \text{relationally coupled} \rightarrow \text{orthogonal organization}$$

Being distinct and coupled is not yet enough for right angles. Two directions sixty degrees apart are also distinct, and they can also be coupled. Relational independence means zero cross-component: a change expressed entirely along one relational direction, u, has zero projection onto the other, v. Book I has earned metric structure, and with it the inner product that measures projection:

$$u\cdot v = |u|\,|v|\cos\theta$$

If $u\cdot v = 0$ for nonzero u and v, then $\cos\theta = 0$, and therefore $\theta = 90^\circ$. The sequence is

$$\boxed{\text{relational independence} \rightarrow \text{zero cross-coupling} \rightarrow \text{zero projection} \rightarrow \text{orthogonal spatial expression}}$$

Independence is not defined as being already orthogonal; the right angle follows from zero relational overlap once the metric exists. A propagating closure preserves that independence because the two modes must remain distinguishable enough to play different roles while coupled enough to remain one process.

The count of polarizations needs one more step. We first condition the ideal free-propagation regime: the region is locally charge-free, sufficiently far from material boundaries and localized sources, outside dominant near-field structure, approximately homogeneous, and without strong imposed anisotropy. Under those conditions the free propagating electromagnetic mode is transverse. In three spatial dimensions (Book I, Chapters 12 and 13), the directions across the travel form a plane, and that plane supports two independent polarization degrees of freedom.

What secures transversality in this ideal regime? In Maxwell's description, a source-free region supports transverse free-propagating modes, while near charges, boundaries and focused structures, and under other non-ideal conditions, longitudinal components can appear. This places a corresponding burden on Chapter 19: the free-propagation regime should emerge only after charge and the environmental conditions that distinguish source-free propagation have been earned.

Measured light carries no net charge. Our proposal (◌) is that this neutrality is a resultant of opposed charge states rather than an absence of charge structure. Section 20.5 allows two co-mingled local cycles before each hand-off: a transformation cycle that informs the neighboring relations, and an update or reconciliation cycle organized by the constraints those neighbors return. If the two cycles carry charge states of equal size and opposite sign, the externally measured charge is their sum:

$$Q_{\mathrm{net}} = q_{\mathrm{transform}} + q_{\mathrm{update}} = 0,\qquad q_{\mathrm{transform}} \neq 0,\quad q_{\mathrm{update}} \neq 0$$

with each charge in coulombs (C). Measured electrical neutrality is then the sum of overlapping opposed charge states, while the internal charge structure stays available for electromagnetic coupling, including coupling between two excitations (20.23). This is a candidate mechanism for Chapter 19 to earn or reject: Chapter 19 must first earn what charge is, and then whether the two local cycles can carry it with opposite signs. The proposal is untested.
