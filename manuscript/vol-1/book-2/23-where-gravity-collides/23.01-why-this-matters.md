# 23.1 Why This Matters

When this book was published, there were two established theories for predicting our modern world, and those theories conflicted in many ways.

The first is Einstein's general relativity, the theory of gravity. It treats space and time as a single flexible fabric that matter bends. It is not an abstraction. The satellites of the Global Positioning System carry atomic clocks, and those clocks run faster than clocks on the ground by about 38 millionths of a second a day: partly because the satellites move fast, and mostly because gravity is weaker at the satellites' altitude. Engineers correct for the difference. If the engineers didn't, a GPS position would drift by roughly ten kilometers a day. Every phone map depends on gravity bending time.

The second is quantum mechanics, the theory of atoms, light and everything small. Lasers, transistors, LEDs, MRI scanners and the atomic clocks in those satellites are all quantum devices. A large share of the modern economy rests on technology that could not have been designed without it.

Each theory has survived extraordinarily stringent tests within the domain where it is used. Yet they describe the world in incompatible terms. General relativity says that every object has a definite place and bends the fabric around it accordingly. Quantum mechanics says that before a measurement, a small object can be in a combination of places at once, a *superposition*. What, then, does the fabric do around an object that is in two places? Nobody knows. That gap is the deepest open problem in physics, and this chapter is about to unpack it.

The stakes are practical as well as philosophical. Quantum computers, quantum sensors and secure quantum communication all work by keeping fragile superpositions alive, and the engineering question underneath them is how large and how long a superposition can be kept. If, as Roger Penrose has argued, gravity sets a limit, then there is a ceiling on what those technologies can do, and finding it would be one of the most consequential measurements of the century. The stakes for our picture of ourselves are as large. If the account in this chapter is right, the solid, separate objects of everyday life are the settled outcomes of a deeper, connected field, and the connections reach further than space does. That picture, of a world made of relations rather than of separate things, is what we explore ahead.
