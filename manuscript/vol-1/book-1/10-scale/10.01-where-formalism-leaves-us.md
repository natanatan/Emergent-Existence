# 10.1 Where Formalism Leaves Us

Formalism lets us hold the earlier ontology in one formal view. Configurations can be distinguished, transitions followed, equivalence classes recognized, and returned identities compared without committing ourselves to geometry.

Now change the question. Place two relational organizations before us and ask not whether they differ, but whether one is larger. The current frame goes silent.

Nothing is wrong with the formalism. We have simply reached the edge of what it resolves. Scale adds a new relational capacity: comparison of magnitude.

▪ 𝔊 = (𝒞, ℜ, 𝒰)

Its routes are relational trajectories:

▪ Σ₀ 𝒰 Σ₁ 𝒰 Σ₂ 𝒰 … 𝒰 Σ<sub>k</sub>

We can already compare kind. We can say that two organizations are equivalent or distinct, that one relation is independent of another, or that a trajectory returns to an identity class. None of this yet tells us how much.

To make magnitude visible, we must resist the ordinary shortcut of importing numbers or a ruler. We first need a relation that can distinguish equal, greater, lesser. Eventually proportionate organization from within the structure itself.

▪ The map is structurally coherent and quantitatively mute

▪ formal comparability ⇏ magnitude

Only after that step will a later chapter be allowed to ask how far. Scale must come before distance.

▪ Formalism’s core supports comparison of kind while supplying nothing about amount. Open question: whether the weakest adequate notion of magnitude is cardinal, as this chapter assumes (Question 1).
