# 23.19 Setting and Testing the Threshold

The proposal makes retention a physical event, so it must say when the event happens and accept that experiments can catch it out.

With the two-ended reading of 23.9, the threshold has a sharper job: it says when an interaction must close. A two-ended consistency rule on its own makes the same predictions as standard quantum mechanics, as the transactional interpretation does, and so it is an interpretation. The incompatibility threshold is what makes it physics, since it fixes when closure is forced and predicts where superpositions of large objects fail. It also answers a question the transactional interpretation leaves open: what counts as a receiving end. On this proposal, a receiving end is any coupling whose alternatives would condition the field past the threshold.

Four quantities have to be fixed. The first is the threshold: the measure of incompatibility (23.14) and its critical value. The second is the timescale: how quickly retention happens once the threshold is crossed. Detector clicks are fast, and the mechanism must reproduce that speed from the relational dynamics. The third is conservation: the feedback must account for all the energy and other conserved quantities of the spread-out field. When the steering of 23.17 gathers the whole quantum at one stripe, nothing is left over. Energy that is not retained in a detection, in an emission that closes at no detector or in a build-up that fails to reach the threshold, does not vanish. It dissipates as heat into the apparatus or its surroundings, below what the apparatus records, and the framework must say where that heat goes. The heat falls under the same heating bounds as the collapse models discussed below, and it is itself something experiments can look for. The fourth is the outcome law of 23.17.

Penrose stated his threshold as a time rather than a sharp cutoff. A superposition of two mass arrangements survives for a time that shrinks as the gravitational energy separating them grows. That time is what ties the threshold to the double slit experiment: the stripes form only if the field stays coherent until it reaches the screen, still in both arrangements with one definite relation between them. When the survival time is far longer than the trip through the apparatus, as for an electron, the stripes appear. When it is shorter, one arrangement is retained along the way and the stripes fade. Experiments therefore test the threshold by holding larger objects in two arrangements for longer and looking for the stripes to fade sooner than decoherence alone predicts.

Every physical collapse proposal predicts small departures from standard quantum mechanics, and those departures are being searched for. Large objects should lose interference faster than decoherence alone predicts. Matter should be slightly warmed, and should emit faint radiation, by the collapse process itself. The 2021 Gran Sasso measurement (Donadi and colleagues) ruled out the simplest, parameter-free version of the Diósi–Penrose model this way, and a 2026 analysis of data from the VIP experiment at the same laboratory did the same for a generalized Károlyházy model (Bortolotti and colleagues, 2026). Each result excludes one tested version, not gravity-related collapse as a whole. Any threshold this framework proposes must lie in the region these experiments leave open. That is a constraint, and it is also an opportunity: a threshold defined from the relational dynamics would predict where the next experiment should look.

There is a further, quantitative burden that the unification of 23.9 carries. Gravity between two protons is weaker than the electric force between them by a factor of about 10³⁶. If gravity and detection-localization are one self-conditioning seen at two scales, the framework has to derive why the two effective strengths differ by such an enormous factor, and show that the large-scale limit agrees with gravitational observations while the small-scale limit reproduces detector statistics. Ordinary gravitational attraction, as general relativity describes it, is far too weak to localize a photon at a screen, and the proposal does not claim that it does. The claim is that both descend from a common, more primitive conditioning.

Gravity's speed adds a constraint of a different kind. The closure of 23.9 sends nothing through space, so the speed of light does not limit it. Changes in gravity, measured at large scale, travel at the speed of light: in 2017, ripples in the curvature of space from a merger of two neutron stars about 130 million light-years away arrived within two seconds of the merger's gamma rays, which matches the two speeds to about one part in 10¹⁵. If gravity is the same conditioning added up over large masses, the framework must show why changes in the accumulated conditioning propagate at exactly the speed of light. ◌ A single closure may build up gradually, below what any apparatus records, so the moment we notice it is not the moment the organizing system began responding. That build-up is not a signal between distant detectors: an entangled pair is one interaction closed through its common source (23.11). Speed does not relieve the strength burden either. A closure as weak as ordinary gravity would still force nothing at a detector.
