# 20.20 Bell's Constraints

The disordered-locality reading of 20.11, and the two-ended interaction of 20.9, have to meet four tests that are already sharply measured.

First, it must reproduce the strength of the quantum correlations exactly. Bell's limit for preset values, in its usual form (Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt), caps a certain combination of correlations at 2. Quantum mechanics reaches up to 2√2, about 2.83, and no further; that ceiling is known as Tsirelson's bound. Theoretical models can be built that exceed it (the "PR boxes" of Popescu and Rohrlich), so a relational mechanism that overshoots would be refuted as surely as one that falls short.

Second, it must not permit signaling. Entangled correlations cannot be used to send a message, and a relational order in which distant organizations are directly connected must explain why the connection carries correlation but not control. The two-ended interaction of 20.9 must meet a stricter version of this test: no signaling into the past. An experimenter who chooses a detector setting now must not be able to send a controllable message to the emitter's past. A constraint from the later end of an interaction is not a signal from it, and the framework must show why its consistency condition allows the first and forbids the second.

Third, the emergent space must keep the symmetries that relativity requires. Lorentz invariance, the requirement that the laws of physics look the same to every observer moving at a steady speed, has been tested to extraordinary precision: light of different energies from a gamma-ray burst in 2009 arrived together after billions of years of travel, ruling out the simplest ways a discrete or relational substrate could break it. Disordered locality must be rare and structured enough to leave those symmetries intact at every scale tested.

Fourth, it must survive delayed choice. In Wheeler's delayed-choice arrangement, and in the quantum-eraser experiments that followed (Kim and colleagues, 2000; Jacques and colleagues, 2007), the choice of what to measure is made after the field has passed the slits. Experiments confirm the quantum predictions, and they never change a record already made: interference appears only when records are sorted afterward by the later measurement. A two-ended account has a natural reading of these results, since the later choice is one end of the interaction. It must reproduce the exact sorted statistics and never rewrite a retained record.

These tests share one weakness, and the chapter names it plainly. Bell's theorem assumes *measurement independence*: the state the source prepares does not depend on the settings the detectors will later use. A two-ended interaction gives that assumption up, since what closes is consistent with both detector settings. That is how this account, like other retrocausal models (Price, 1996; Wharton and Argaman, 2020), stays outside Bell's limit, and it is the account's weakest point. Disordered locality and the two-ended interaction are not two escapes but one: relational adjacency that need not follow either the spatial order or the temporal order. There are different ways to give up the assumption. *Superdeterministic* models correlate the source and the settings through common prior conditions, and critics object that such models could explain almost any correlation after the fact. Retrocausal models instead allow later settings to constrain the earlier state of the same interaction. The proposal here belongs to the second family, and the framework must show that its loss is lawful and narrow: that it follows from the consistency condition across one interaction, yields exactly the quantum correlations and nothing more, and never lets anyone predict or control the settings from the source.
