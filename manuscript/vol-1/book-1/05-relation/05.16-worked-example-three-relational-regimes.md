# 5.16 Worked Example: Three Relational Regimes

A minimal example separates coexistence, connectivity and structure. It is non-geometric; the indices are labels only.

| **Regime** | **Relations** | **Key condition** | **Interpretation** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Coexistence | r<sub>01</sub>, r<sub>23</sub> | No shared relata; no mutual constraint | Two retained relations coexist but do not form one integrated structure |
| B. Connectivity | r<sub>01</sub>, r<sub>12</sub> | Shared q<sub>1</sub>, but 𝒫(r<sub>01</sub> given r<sub>12</sub>) = 𝒫(r<sub>01</sub>) | A network exists, but shared participation alone does not make the relations mutually constraining |
| C. Structure | r<sub>01</sub>, r<sub>12</sub>, r<sub>20</sub> | r<sub>20</sub> = f(r<sub>01</sub>, r<sub>12</sub>) | At least one relation is restricted by the organization of the others; the domain is integrated |

Now perturb one relation in Regime C, r<sub>01</sub> ↦ r′<sub>01</sub>. Several outcomes are possible. The structure may collapse, revealing that its organization was fragile. It may reorganize into a new structure with a different invariant. Or it may absorb the perturbation while preserving the same identity-relevant invariant, F(ℛ′) = F(ℛ). The third case is the strongest candidate for object-like persistence.

It also supplies an obvious candidate for the computational companion. Construct weighted relational networks, perturb edges or constraints, identify candidate clusters, and measure whether identity-relevant organization survives. The network-robustness literature (Albert, Jeong and Barabási, 2000) and community-detection methods (Newman and Girvan, 2004) offer ready-made instruments, with the usual guardrail. The simulation cannot prove that physical objects arise this way. It can test whether the proposed criterion distinguishes persistent integrated structures from arbitrary collections of relations.
