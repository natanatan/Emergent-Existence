# 1.17 Precursors and Departures

The possibility that an origin may remain unresolved without being equivalent to either absolute nothing or perfect uniformity has historical precedents.

**Precursors.** Anaximander’s *apeiron* was described as a boundless or indefinite source from which differentiated opposites emerge. The important precursor here is not the claim that Ω is an *apeiron*, but the older idea that determinacy need not be fully present at the beginning.

Charles Sanders Peirce later developed an evolutionary cosmology in which determinacy and law are not simply treated as eternally fixed features of reality. His writings on chance, continuity, and habit explore the possibility that regularity itself can emerge from a more indeterminate condition.

These two traditions approach the problem from very different directions, but together they bracket a question Origin deliberately leaves open: whether the absence of resolved distinction reflects genuine uniformity, or whether variation may exist below the level at which anything has yet become stably distinguishable.

**Departure.** The framework makes a thinner claim than either precursor. Ω is not asserted to be an indefinite substance, a reservoir of latent opposites, a field of chance, or a productive cause. Nor is it assigned any tendency to generate order. Origin assumes no physical time in which a process of differentiation, habit formation, or cosmogenesis could unfold.

Ω is only the minimally resolved reference condition from which no operative distinction is available within the framework’s initial resolving frame.

The framework therefore leaves both possibilities open:

▪ no lower-order variation exists

▪ lower-order variation exists but remains unresolved

Neither possibility is currently earned.

What matters at Origin is not what Ω secretly contains, but what the framework is entitled to resolve from it. Later chapters must earn persistence, organization, closure, orientation, return, and the regularities that make stable structure possible rather than placing them into the starting condition.

In that sense, Anaximander and Peirce are precursors to the question, not premises of the answer.

Questions Opened by This Framework

Is Ω unresolved because no lower-order variation exists, or because variation exists below the framework’s initial resolving capacity?

Can Ω ultimately be eliminated as an entity-like term and replaced entirely by the condition that no operative distinction is resolved?

Does the conceptual descent identify genuine ontological dependencies, or only the order in which human cognition finds them easiest to remove?

Is distinction genuinely primitive, or can a still weaker relation generate it?

Why does differentiation become operative at all?

Can later origin roles be earned from closure and higher-order addressability without copying Ω forward as a privileged anchor?

Can relation be specified without importing ordering, metric, locality, topology, or geometry?

Can an origin role be re-instantiated at higher organizational levels without smuggling in a hierarchy as a primitive?

What would falsify the claim that the later geometric structure has been earned rather than encoded in the starting relations?

Where does derivational necessity end and contingent structural selection begin?

If Volume I reaches geometry, what additional structures are required before that geometry deserves a physical spacetime interpretation?

For information as a difference that makes a difference, cited here for the family of intuitions rather than for the later formal account.

Leibniz, G. W. (1714). *Principles of Nature and Grace, Based on Reason*, §7. Written in French for Prince Eugene of Savoy; English translation by Jonathan Bennett freely available at earlymoderntexts.com. For the question why there is something rather than nothing.

von Neumann, J. (1923). Zur Einführung der transfiniten Zahlen. *Acta Litterarum ac Scientiarum Regiae Universitatis Hungaricae Francisco-Josephinae, Sectio Scientiarum Mathematicarum* (Szeged), 1, 199–208. For the construction of the natural numbers from the empty set, used here only as a logical analogy.

Parmenides of Elea (c. 475 BCE). *On Nature*, fragments B2 and B6, numbered as in Diels and Kranz, *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker*. Burnet’s English translation of the fragments is in the public domain, in *Early Greek Philosophy* (A. & C. Black, 1908; Project Gutenberg ebook 67097), and is also available arranged by Diels–Kranz number at lexundria.com. For the maxim that nothing comes from nothing.

Spencer-Brown, G. (1969). *Laws of Form*. London: George Allen & Unwin. Reissued by Julian Press (New York, 1972) and in several later editions with added prefaces; the pagination differs between them. For the instruction to draw a distinction as the minimal generative act.
