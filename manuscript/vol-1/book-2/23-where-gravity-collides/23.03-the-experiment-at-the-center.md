# 23.3 The Experiment at the Center

One experiment holds the heart of the quantum puzzle, and the rest of this chapter returns to it often.

***The Double Slit Experiment:*** shine a light at a barrier with two narrow slits, and put a screen behind it. The light waves passing through the slits spread out and overlap. Where the waves from the two slits arrive in sync, they reinforce each other and the screen is bright. Where they arrive out of sync, they negate each other and the screen is dark. The result is a pattern of stripes, known as an *interference pattern*. Thomas Young showed this in the early 1800s, and Young's result settled, for a century, that light behaved like a wave.

Now throttle the light's emission until the source sends one photon at a time. The screen no longer glows. It records single point interactions, each one where the light collides with the detector. What initially looks like individual particles eventually accumulates over thousands of trials, forming the same interference stripes. Each photon lands at one point, yet the pattern of where they land shows that something passed through both slits.

The same phenomenon happens with electrons as well as with whole molecules. Electron detections were filmed building up one at a time in 1989. In 1999 a team in Vienna sent buckyballs, soccer-ball-shaped molecules of 60 carbon atoms, through a grating and witnessed an interference pattern. In 2019 the same Vienna group, led by Markus Arndt, did it again, that time with molecules of up to 2,000 atoms.

Here is the part that has troubled physicists for a century. Put a detector at the slits that records which slit each particle went through, and the stripes disappear. The dots then pile up in a smooth spread, as if the particles had been small bullets all along. Recording the difference between the two slits changes the pattern.

Three facts from this one experiment frame everything that follows. The light passes through both slits and combines with itself. Each trial nevertheless ends at a single point. And recording which slit was involved alters how the propagating light combines.
