# 9.10 Quotient Structure and Higher-Order Identity

If ~<sub>F</sub> partitions admissible configurations into equivalence classes, let QF(Σ) = \[Σ\]<sub>~F</sub> and write 𝒞/~<sub>F</sub> for the quotient collection. Closure interprets a dynamically persistent class O<sub>F</sub> = \[Σ\]<sub>~F</sub> as a candidate higher-order identity and, where representative-independent addressability is available, that identity can function in the organizational anchor role A<sub>F</sub>. Formalism now asks whether admissible succession also descends to those classes.

▪ \[Σ\]<sub>~F</sub> 𝒰̄ \[Σ′\]<sub>F</sub> whenever Σ 𝒰 Σ′

For this to be legitimate, choosing a different representative of \[Σ\]<sub>~F</sub> must not change the higher-order result in a way that destroys the quotient description. That gives the chapter its strongest candidate formalization of higher-order autonomy. When lower-order realizations form an equivalence class and admissible transformations descend consistently to that class, the higher-order identity supports dynamics independent of any arbitrary choice of lower-order representative.

▪ quotient-compatible dynamics ⇒ higher-order formal autonomy

That is stronger than saying quotienting is a convenient description and weaker than claiming all genuine emergence must be quotient structure. The chapter adopts the middle claim: quotient-compatible dynamics is sufficient for a form of higher-order autonomy. Is not asserted to be necessary for every emergent phenomenon.

▪ Emergence acquires a testable criterion rather than a metaphor, and 9.27 shows the criterion failing on a small example. Open question: whether approximate rather than exact quotient autonomy can be formalized once comparison is earned (Question 3).
