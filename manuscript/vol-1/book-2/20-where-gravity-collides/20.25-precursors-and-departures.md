# 20.25 Precursors and Departures

The ingredients here are not new, and several traditions are close.

Penrose (1996, 2014) and Diósi (1987, 1989) proposed that gravity limits superposition, and this chapter adopts their direction. Tilloy and Diósi (2016) proposed that gravity is sourced by collapse events rather than by the averaged quantum state, the closest predecessor to 20.9's claim that observed gravitational effects are such collisions added up. Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber (1986) and later continuous collapse models made collapse a physical process with testable consequences.

Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics (1996) treats quantum states as relative to interacting systems, the closest single predecessor to 20.7. Zurek's work on decoherence and "quantum Darwinism" explains how many observers come to agree on one classical record. Reconstruction programs (Hardy, 2001; Chiribella, D'Ariano and Perinotti, 2011; Masanes and Müller, 2011) derive quantum theory from operational principles.

De Broglie (1924) gave each particle an internal periodic process, the precursor of 20.4's reading of phase. De Broglie's pilot-wave theory (1927), developed by Bohm (1952), kept the wave physically real and needed no collapse; this chapter keeps the real wave but not the particle on a definite path, nor the instantaneous guidance. Huygens (1690) treated every point a wave reaches as a new source of waves, the picture behind each slit sending out its own waves in Figure 20.1. Couder and Fort (2006) and Bush (2015) built a classical model in which walking oil droplets are steered by the waves they make, though later droplet runs did not reproduce clean double slit stripes (Andersen et al., 2015). Duane (1923) explained diffraction stripes as momentum exchanged in whole quanta with the periodic structure of the slits, an early form of the claim that the slit walls decide where each detection lands; 20.17 differs in making the deciding factor each emission's phase as it meets the walls, measured against the detector's answering wave. Lamb and Scully (1969) showed that the photoelectric effect, long taken as proof of photons, follows from a field acting on detector atoms that absorb energy only in quanta, and Lamb (1995) argued against the photon as an object; this is the precedent for 20.10's account of the dots as set by the detector's threshold.

Markopoulou and Smolin (2007) introduced disordered locality. Two-ended accounts go back to Wheeler and Feynman's absorber theory of radiation (1945). Costa de Beauregard (1953) explained the EPR correlations by a path running back to the source and forward again. Aharonov, Bergmann and Lebowitz (1964) described quantum states fixed at both ends of an interval. Cramer's transactional interpretation (1986), developed by Kastner (2012), pairs an emitter's forward wave with an absorber's backward echo. Mead (2000) built electromagnetism from waves exchanged between emitter and absorber, with no photon in transit, and Kastner (2014) argued that spacetime itself is the network of completed transactions. Price (1996) and Wharton and Argaman (2020) argue that retrocausal models can explain Bell correlations without action at a distance. Maudlin (1994) gave the sharpest objection to such accounts.

The framework's departure, if it succeeds, is continuity. Distinction, retention, closure, return, fields, geometry and gravitational conditioning are developed in order, before the measurement problem is reached, and the chapter asks whether measurement can be understood as the same relational organization conditioning itself, rather than as a new substance or an added rule. Three examples show what continuity means here. Wheeler and Feynman, and Cramer after them, added a backward-running wave as a postulate; in this framework the two-ended interaction follows from Chapter 14, where time is order recovered from retained dependency, so an unretained interaction has no internal before and after for an influence to run against. Markopoulou and Smolin introduced disordered locality as a feature of a model; here space was derived from relational steps in Chapters 11 to 13, so places where the relational order and the spatial order come apart are expected rather than added. Penrose, Diósi, and Tilloy and Diósi chose gravity as what ends superposition, by argument from general relativity; here Chapter 19 had already made gravity the conditioning by retained organizations, so the same conditioning acting at a detector is the next step rather than a new hypothesis. In each case the precursors made a choice that this framework inherits from its earlier chapters. Whether those inheritances hold, and are not merely compatible with the earlier chapters, is what 20.22 tests. Each comparison should be kept at the right level: measured result, effective formalism, proposed mechanism, or interpretation. Shared predictions do not establish shared ontology, and shared ontology does not excuse a failure to recover the predictions.
