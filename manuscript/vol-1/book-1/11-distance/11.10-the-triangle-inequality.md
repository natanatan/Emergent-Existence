# 11.10 The Triangle Inequality

One defining property of ordinary metric distance is:

▪ d(A,C) ≤ d(A,B) + d(B,C)

Here it should not be assumed. Suppose the least chain from A to B has magnitude d(A,B), and the least chain from B to C has magnitude d(B,C). Concatenating them yields an admissible chain from A to C. If chain magnitude is additive, its magnitude is d(A,B)+d(B,C). But d(A,C) is defined as the minimum over all admissible chains connecting A and C.

Therefore:

▪ d(A,C) ≤ d(A,B) + d(B,C)

The triangle inequality is not inserted as a geometric axiom. It follows from:

chain composition + additive magnitude + minimality

This is one of the strongest formal results available to Distance, and its premise should be stated with it: the composition step assumes that mediating burdens add. Section 11.8 records that additivity is earned only where the organization supports concatenation without interference, so the triangle inequality inherits that Provisional status. Where composition is non-additive, a weaker inequality may survive, and that is an open question rather than a result.
