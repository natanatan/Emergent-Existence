# 20.2 From Candles to Fields: How Light Was Understood

The modern picture of light was assembled over three centuries by people who often disagreed about what they were measuring.

Christiaan Huygens (1690) treated light as a disturbance spreading through a medium, each point the disturbance reaches acting as a new source. Isaac Newton (1704) favored streams of small corpuscles, which explained straight-line travel and sharp shadows. Thomas Young (1802, 1804) showed that light passing two openings produces bright and dark bands, a pattern that only overlapping waves were then known to produce. Étienne-Louis Malus (1809) found that light reflected from glass acquires an orientation: a filter turned through an angle θ passes a fraction cos²θ of that light.

Michael Faraday found in 1831 that a changing magnetic field drives an electric current, and in 1845 that a magnetic field rotates the orientation of light passing through glass, the first sign that light and magnetism belong together. In 1856, Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch measured the ratio between the electrical and magnetic strengths of the same charge and found that it has the units of a speed, close to the measured speed of light†. James Clerk Maxwell (1865) built on that coincidence: a changing electric field drives a magnetic field and a changing magnetic field drives an electric one, so the pair can sustain each other while travelling, at a speed fixed by those two strengths. Heinrich Hertz (1887) produced such travelling disturbances with electrical sparks, at radio wavelengths.

In the same year, Albert Michelson and Edward Morley looked for a change in the speed of light as Earth moves through the supposed medium and found none. Hendrik Lorentz (1904) and Henri Poincaré answered that the medium might exist while every ruler and clock moving through it changes in exactly the way that hides the motion. Albert Einstein (1905) removed the medium and made the constancy of light's speed a starting principle.

Max Planck (1900) found that the radiation inside a hot cavity is described correctly only if energy is exchanged with its walls in amounts proportional to frequency. Einstein (1905) extended this to light ejecting electrons from metals, and Robert Millikan (1916) measured the straight-line relation between the light's frequency and the electrons' energy. Arthur Compton (1923) showed that light scattered by electrons delivers momentum as well as energy. Richard Beth (1936) showed that circularly polarized light delivers rotational push. Willis Lamb and Marlan Scully (1969) then showed that the photoelectric results follow from a continuous field acting on detector atoms that can only take energy in fixed steps, so the discreteness may belong to the interaction rather than to the light.

Each of these steps added a measurement that any account of light must still meet. Together they leave the deeper question open: what organization is doing all of this?
