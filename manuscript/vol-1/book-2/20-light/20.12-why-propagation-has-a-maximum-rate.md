# 20.12 Why Propagation Has a Maximum Rate

Even in what we perceive as empty space, the region into which light propagates is continually being updated (20.4). An incoming excitation therefore enters a neighborhood that already has a relational state. For the excitation to continue, its completed state must be reconciled with that state and with the relations that keep the neighborhood consistent through time:

$$\text{incoming completed state} \rightarrow \text{local reconciliation} \rightarrow \text{neighborhood integration} \rightarrow \text{next re-instantiation}$$

Completing that reconciliation takes time. Spatial hand-off cannot occur until the local state has been updated and reconciled with the neighboring region. One local cycle may suffice, or several local turns may contribute before a transfer becomes admissible; what matters for propagation is the total period required to complete one coherent hand-off. That gives propagation an upper limit (◌):

$$c = \frac{d_{\mathrm{handoff}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{effective\ handoff}}}$$

where $d_{\mathrm{handoff}}$ is the effective distance one hand-off advances the excitation (metres, m) and $\tau_{\mathrm{effective\ handoff}}$ is the full latency of one hand-off: local cycling, reconciliation and transfer together (seconds, s). Their ratio is a speed (m/s). Because one local turn is not identified with one spatial step, the period of a single local cycle does not by itself fix c.

On this reading, c is a property of the relational medium through which luminous organization is handed from neighborhood to neighborhood. The measured limit is already known; our proposal concerns the mechanism that can generate that limit. We are not yet deriving the numerical value 299,792,458 m/s.

One consistency requirement remains, because measurement binds every account: c must come out the same in every direction and for every observer in uniform motion. Our ontological proposal is that clocks, rulers, matter and luminous propagation are constituted within the same relational medium and therefore transform under the same underlying update constraints. Their measured relations remain mutually coordinated because the measuring systems and the propagating excitation share the same substrate dynamics. Section 20.19 shows how that proposal maps onto the measured slowing of moving clocks.
