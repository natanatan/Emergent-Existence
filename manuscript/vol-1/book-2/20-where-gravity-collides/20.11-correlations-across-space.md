# 20.11 Correlations Across Space

There is one more fact any account of quantum behavior has to face, and it is the strangest.

In 1935 Einstein, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen pointed out that quantum mechanics allows two particles to be prepared together so that their properties stay linked however far apart they travel. Measure one, and you know what a matching measurement on the other will show. Einstein concluded that the particles must have carried definite values all along, like a pair of gloves separated into two boxes: open one box, find a left glove, and you know the other holds the right. The alternative, he thought, was "spooky action at a distance."

In 1964 John Bell showed that the question could be settled by experiment. If the particles carry preset values, as the gloves do, then the correlations between measurements made at different settings cannot exceed a certain limit, whatever those values are. Quantum mechanics predicts that the correlations exceed this limit. Beginning in the 1970s, and decisively in 2015, with every known loophole closed at once, experiments confirmed that they do. The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to John Clauser, Alain Aspect and Anton Zeilinger for this work. The correlations cannot be explained by values fixed in advance and carried along through space, nor by any signal traveling between the particles at or below the speed of light. Yet they cannot be used to send a message faster than light either.

Most attempts to explain quantum behavior with a deeper mechanism break here, because any mechanism that works locally *in space* cannot produce these correlations. This framework has an answer of its own, because in this framework space is not fundamental. Chapters 11 to 13 derived distance, geometry and space from relations: two organizations are close when few steps of mediation connect them. Space is a large-scale summary of those relations, and like every summary it can miss things. Two organizations can be one relational step apart while far apart in the space that emerges.

That is what entangled particles are: organizations that remain directly related in the underlying relational order while being far apart in emergent space. The correlation is local where it actually happens, in the relational order. It only looks like action at a distance when projected into space. The physicists Fotini Markopoulou and Lee Smolin called this *disordered locality*: the underlying order and the spatial order mostly agree, and in a few places they come apart. On this reading, Bell's result is evidence that the world is more connected than its geometry shows. The two-ended interaction of 20.9 says the same thing about time. Relational adjacency need not follow temporal order any more than spatial order. An entangled pair is one interaction closed through its common source, the path Olivier Costa de Beauregard proposed in 1953; drawn in spacetime, it runs back to the source and forward to the other detector, and nothing in it travels faster than light. What the source sets is not a pair of preset values, as with the gloves: the outcome at each detector is the closure consistent with the source and with both detectors' settings.
