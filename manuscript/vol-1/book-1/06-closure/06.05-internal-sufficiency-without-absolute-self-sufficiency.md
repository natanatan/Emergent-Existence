# 6.5 Internal Sufficiency Without Absolute Self-Sufficiency

The most dangerous phrase in our definition is “internally sufficient.” Taken too strongly, it would exclude every system that depends on an environment. Taken too weakly, it would permit any externally scaffolded collection to call itself closed.

The distinction should therefore be made at the level of constitutive dependency. Suppose ℛ requires an input x from outside the candidate domain. Two cases differ.

If x supplies a condition under which the organization operates but does not itself determine the organization that constitutes F, x may be an environmental input without defeating closure with respect to F.

If x supplies one of the dependencies without which the identity-relevant organization F is not specified or maintained, the candidate domain is not closed with respect to F unless x is included in the domain.

This is analogous, but not identical, to the distinction made in the theory of reflexively autocatalytic and food-generated (RAF) sets (Hordijk, Steel and Kauffman, 2019). An RAF set can be self-sustaining with respect to its internal catalytic organization while still depending on an ambient food set. RAF theory therefore supplies a useful counterexample to the idea that closure means total external independence. It also supplies a second warning: RAF closure does not automatically include a boundary. Hordijk and Steel explicitly note that boundary is not part of the basic RAF formalism and must be added or modeled separately (Hordijk and Steel, 2015).

The lesson for this framework is precise: internal organizational sufficiency does not entail environmental isolation. Network self-support does not by itself entail boundary.
