# 23.4 Where Quantum Behavior Begins

If molecules of 2,000 atoms can build interference stripes, how come baseballs repeatedly thrown through two narrow slits never build them?

The answer starts with a proposal Louis de Broglie made in 1924, as a doctoral student. He suggested that every particle, not just light, is accompanied by a wave, and that the wave's length depends on the particle's momentum (its mass times its velocity):

λ = h / p

Here λ (lambda) is the wavelength, *h* is Planck's constant, and *p* is the momentum. Because *h* is so small, the wavelength is tiny for anything heavy or fast. An electron moving at a few thousand kilometers per second has a wavelength of about a tenth of a billionth of a meter, about the spacing of atoms in a crystal, which is why crystals make electrons interfere (first seen by Davisson and Germer in 1927). Work the same formula for a baseball thrown at 90 miles an hour and its wavelength comes out at about 10⁻³⁴ meters. This is not the length of the throw, but the spacing of the ripples within it: ten billion billion times smaller than a single proton. We can therefore conclude that as the mass of an object increases, its wavelength correspondingly decreases.

---

**Increasing Mass ⇒ Decreasing Wavelength**

---

> **Notation: the de Broglie wavelength**
>
> λ = h / p, with p = m × v
>
> λ is the wavelength, in meters (m). *h* is Planck's constant, about 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds (J·s). *p* is the momentum, in kilogram-meters per second (kg·m/s): the mass *m*, in kilograms (kg), times the speed *v*, in meters per second (m/s).
>
> Mass sits below the line. At the same speed, doubling the mass halves the wavelength.
>
> - An electron (9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg) at 7,000 km/s: about 10⁻¹⁰ m, the spacing of atoms in a crystal.
> - A buckyball (1.2 × 10⁻²⁴ kg) at 220 m/s: about 2.5 × 10⁻¹² m.
> - A baseball (0.145 kg) at 40 m/s (90 miles an hour): about 10⁻³⁴ m.

Interference shows up when an object's own wavelength is comparable to the features it passes through: the width of the slits, and the spacing between them. That is why the Vienna molecules had to be slowed and sent through gratings whose slits were a few hundred billionths of a meter apart. Send baseballs through slits wide enough for them to pass, and any stripes would lie about 10⁻³³ meters apart, far too fine for any screen to show. Spreading them out enough to see would take slits smaller than an atomic nucleus, which no baseball could pass through. That is why thrown baseballs would never build interference stripes.

So quantum behavior has a definite scale, set by each object's own wavelength: the distance over which the object's cycle changes appreciably. Whether that cycle shows in an experiment then depends on the apparatus around the object: the size of its openings, how long the field stays coherent, and what else couples to the field along the way. Quantum behavior does not begin at the Planck length, and it does not need spacetime to be anything but smooth. As early as 1975, neutrons in an interferometer showed a quantum phase shift caused by ordinary Earth gravity.

In this framework's terms, the de Broglie wavelength marks the scale at which a field's alternatives are *unresolved* by the arrangement it passes through. Chapter 8 (Return) earned a form of cyclic organization: re-entry into the same identity class without repeating the same state. A position within such a cycle is what physicists call a *phase*, a point on a circle. De Broglie himself pictured each particle as carrying an internal clock, and this framework reads that clock as Return: the wavelength is the spatial expression of that cycle. Where two routes through an apparatus differ in length by about a wavelength or less, their cycles can arrive in sync or out of sync, and the field combines with itself. Where the routes differ by vastly more, as for a baseball, the cycles are too fine to matter and only the average survives.
