# 10.4 Pairing

Take two collections of distinguishable members. Do not count them. Pair them instead, one against one.

Let A and B be finite relational organizations whose relevant members are distinguishable under the declared criterion. If every member of A matches exactly one member of B and every member of B is matched, the pairing is a bijection.

▪ f : A ↔ B

Something has been learned before any numeral: the two organizations have the same cardinal magnitude.

▪ A ~# B

Read plainly, A and B are the same size in the cardinal sense. This is because neither leaves a member unmatched when paired with the other. This is the first quantitative landmark in the chapter and it is still relational. No unit has been introduced and nothing has been measured.

Equinumerosity behaves as an equivalence relation, so eligible organizations group into classes sharing a magnitude.

▪ A ~# A; A ~# B ⇒ B ~# A; A ~# B ∧ B ~# C ⇒ A ~# C

We can now say “the same amount” before we possess the number that names it.

▪ Sameness of magnitude is definable by correspondence alone, with no numeral and no unit. Open question: whether the declared membership criterion can itself be constrained independently (10.28, and Question 5).
