# 23.5 A Field Before Particles

The usual telling of the double slit experiment takes the perspective of a particle and asks which slit it went through. This chapter instead starts from the perspective of the field.

Chapter 16 earned *relational fields*: organizations spread across many locations whose parts constrain one another, so that what happens in one region depends on the state of the whole. A field in this sense is not a substance poured into space. It is a pattern of dependence, and space itself is one of the structures such patterns organize (Chapters 12 and 13).

What leaves the source in the double slit experiment is such a field, spread across the apparatus. The two slits are two openings the apparatus cuts into it, not two roads something travels. Labels like "slit A" and "slit B" describe the apparatus, and they need not describe the field passing through. What the field carries across the apparatus is the relation between its parts: where each part is in its cycle relative to the others. While nothing in the apparatus records the difference between the openings, that relation survives to the screen.

Physicists call a state in which the parts keep a definite relation across the whole *coherent*, and a combination of alternatives held together that way a *superposition*. In this framework a superposition is the ordinary condition of a field whose alternatives have not been resolved. It is the general case. The question this chapter has to answer is therefore not why the small world is strange, but why the large world is not: why, past some threshold, only a single outcome is observed.
