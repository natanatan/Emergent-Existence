# 1.4 The Limit of Subtraction

Conceptual subtraction can only take us so far.

We can remove assumed objects, locations, durations, observers, causes, laws, and even resolved differences. But subtraction itself does not prove that what remains is literally nothing. It tells us only that the framework has reached the point where it is no longer entitled to specify further structure.

That distinction matters because “nothing” is not a neutral word. Absolute nothing would mean not merely an empty region or an unoccupied state, but the absence of entities, relations, laws, possibilities, locations, times, and any structure by which one condition could differ from another. Once such a condition is assigned a role in an argument, as a state, source, substrate, reference, or cause, it has already been given more structure than absolute nothing permits.

Nor do familiar scientific and mathematical uses of emptiness solve the problem. An empty set belongs to a formal system. A vacuum belongs to a physical theory. A model without classical spacetime may still contain mathematical relations, dynamical constraints, or admissible alternatives. Each can be extremely sparse without being structureless.

The descent therefore reaches neither a hidden primordial object nor a demonstrated metaphysical void.

It reaches a limit on what the framework can resolve.

What the framework can do is choose a starting condition weak enough not to settle that question in advance.

The constructive ascent will begin from there: not from a claim about what ultimate reality must secretly be, but from the least committed condition from which a first Difference can be introduced without borrowing the richer structures the book is trying to derive.

That reference condition is what this book calls Origin, written as Ω.
