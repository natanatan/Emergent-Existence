# 7.10 What Selects One Branch?

If two candidate orientations 𝔒 and 𝔒⁻¹ are initially exchange-related, what makes one persist? The best answer available here is conditional rather than universal. Recursive return can break their dynamical equivalence even when the underlying generative rule privileges neither branch in advance.

▪ Ret(A<sub>F</sub>; 𝔒) ≠ Ret(A<sub>F</sub>; 𝔒⁻¹)

If one branch preserves or recovers F while its reverse does not, the two cease to be equivalent relative to the higher-order identity. Re-entry compatibility can therefore differentially stabilize asymmetries after they arise. This does not explain every possible first fluctuation. The weaker result is enough: the rule need not encode which orientation will survive, only permit relational variation, after which higher-order closure and identity re-entry discriminate among the resulting branches.

◌ SPECULATIVE Symmetry breaking supplies candidate asymmetries; compatibility with recursive closure and anchor-relative return can differentially stabilize some asymmetric dependency structures, producing derived orientation without a primitive preferred direction.

Equivariant bifurcation theory supplies a mathematical analogue, in that symmetric dynamical laws can possess lower-symmetry solution branches whose symmetry type is constrained by the system’s group action (Golubitsky, Stewart and Schaeffer, 1988; Gatermann, 2000). The present proposal is not an application of the equivariant branching lemma. It identifies a family of tools worth testing in the computational companion.
