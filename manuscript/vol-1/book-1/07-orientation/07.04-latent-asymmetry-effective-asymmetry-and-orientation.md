# 7.4 Latent Asymmetry, Effective Asymmetry and Orientation

Three thresholds are worth separating, since ordinary language collapses them into “symmetry breaking”.

Latent asymmetry. A non-equivalence exists at some finer relational description but does not survive the resolving criterion of the higher-order identity under consideration.

Effective asymmetry. The non-equivalence survives the higher-order resolving map and changes the admissible organization at that level.

Orientation. The effective asymmetry becomes organized as a persistent asymmetric dependency whose consequences remain available through enough recursive transformation to affect re-entry relative to the organizational anchor A<sub>F</sub>.

Let W⁽ᴸ⁾ be a resolving frame associated with organizational level L, in the sense introduced in Boundary. A lower-order difference may be unresolved at L:

▪ W⁽ᴸ⁾(Σ) = W⁽ᴸ⁾(gΣ)

for a relevant exchange or reversal transformation g. Recursive interaction can nevertheless amplify or integrate lower-order differences into an organization for which:

▪ W⁽ᴸ⁾(Σ*) ≠ W⁽ᴸ⁾(gΣ*)

The difference has crossed the resolution threshold of the higher-order organization. The transition is not merely observational if the resolved difference changes the system’s admissible relational transformations. That is the point at which asymmetry becomes dynamically effective.

Renormalization-group and critical-phenomena research supplies an established analogue for the general idea that effective descriptions and symmetries can depend on scale, and that collective behavior can acquire properties not transparent in microscopic variables (Fisher, 1974; Hohenberg and Halperin, 1977; Metzner and colleagues, 2012). We borrow only that methodological lesson. W⁽ᴸ⁾ is not identified with a renormalization-group transformation, and no energy or length scale is assumed. The coarse-graining operator CG\[·\] belongs later in Scale; resolution here means only frame-relative discriminatory access as earned in Boundary.
