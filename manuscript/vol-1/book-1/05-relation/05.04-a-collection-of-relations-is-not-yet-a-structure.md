# 5.4 A Collection of Relations Is Not Yet a Structure

The existence of several relations is insufficient. Two unrelated retained distinctions can coexist without forming one integrated structure. Even connectivity is too weak: two relations may share a state-position while remaining freely variable with respect to each other. A stronger threshold is needed.

Boundary (4.10) introduced 𝒫 for the set of relational states reachable from a configuration. Let 𝒫(r<sub>ij</sub>) be the admissible states of a relation considered by itself, and 𝒫(r<sub>ij</sub> \| ℛ) the admissible states of the same relation embedded in the wider domain. If the surrounding organization restricts what r<sub>ij</sub> can be, then:

▪ 𝒫(r<sub>ij</sub> \| ℛ) ≠ 𝒫(r<sub>ij</sub>)

The difference between these two possibility sets is the first formal sign that the relation participates in an integrated structure. The larger domain now matters to the local relation. This yields the chapter’s working definition.

A relational structure is a set of retained relations whose admissible states are constrained by the organization of other relations in the set.

Constraint here is deliberately broader than causation. It may be logical, combinatorial, algebraic, dependency-based or eventually dynamical. We do not need physical causation to say that one relational configuration excludes another. Causal interpretation is a later achievement. Nor must every relation constrain every other. Structure may be modular: one subset tightly integrated, another only weakly coupled. That possibility matters once object-like substructures are introduced.

▪ Mutual constraint, defined as a difference between 𝒫(r<sub>ij</sub>) and 𝒫(r<sub>ij</sub> \| ℛ), separates structure from mere connectivity without invoking causation. Open question: whether the constraint relation can be formalized without probabilistic or metric language (Question 1).
