# 20.7 Measurement Is a Physical Act

In this framework, measuring a structure means coupling to it physically, so that a difference in that structure affects the observation detected by the measuring device. A detector is not a spectator standing outside the world. It is an organized system that becomes correlated with the distinctions in a field by interacting with it.

Every measurement has a cost. To tell two structures apart, the measuring system has to interact with them strongly enough that their difference shows up in it. When the object being measured is large and stable, such as a ruler on a table read by the light bouncing off it, the cost is negligible, and measurement can be treated as simply reading a value that was already there. When the structure being measured is small and delicate, such as a single field passing through two slits, the interaction needed to tell its parts apart is large compared with what holds those parts in their relation, and measuring changes the structure it measures.

Here is a plain account of what happens when a detector attempts to measure which slit contains the propagating element. The slit detector breaks the symmetry between the two openings. Its interaction with the field decoheres the phase relation between the two slits: each part of the field is now tied to a different state of the slit detector, so the parts no longer arrive at the screen with one definite relation between their cycles. The effect is graded: the stronger the detector's probe, the fainter the stripes, until at full strength the screen records none.

What decides whether a measurement merely reads or also reshapes is a comparison: how hard the measurement has to push, against how firmly the structure being measured holds together. Size is one ingredient in that comparison, which is why the quantum world looks small. It is not the whole of it. A large, cold, well-isolated object can hold a superposition; a single atom battered by its surroundings cannot.
